# The question

Where does rain run off a roof or paved surface in your area, and could a rain
garden safely receive some of it? A useful local answer needs to identify the
runoff source, the receiving area, soil and drainage conditions, the route of
overflow, nearby buildings and utilities, maintenance responsibility, and
applicable local rules. Do not post an exact home address or another person's
location to answer it.

# What the sources support

The [US Environmental Protection Agency's green infrastructure guide](https://www.epa.gov/green-infrastructure/types-green-infrastructure)
describes rain gardens as shallow planted areas that collect and filter
stormwater runoff. Its [rain garden resource index](https://www.epa.gov/soakuptherain/soak-rain-rain-gardens)
points to regional guides for siting, design, installation, and maintenance.
EPA labels that resource index as general information that is no longer being
updated, so a local guide and current local rules still need checking.
Those sources support considering a rain garden as a stormwater practice. They
do not establish that a particular property is suitable or that a Turntide
installation has reduced flooding.

# A measured result elsewhere

The [US Geological Survey's Gary, Indiana study](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/stormwater-reduction-and-water-budget-a-rain-garden-sandy-soil-gary-indiana-2016-18)
monitored a City Hall parking-area intervention before and after construction
of a central rain garden over sandy soil during the spring, summer, and fall of
2016–18. USGS reports that the share of study-area precipitation discharged to
the sewer fell from about 25 percent before construction to 2 percent after,
an estimated 80.3 percent reduction in stormwater discharged to that sewer.
The study measured that site and intervention; it does not predict the effect
of a different garden, soil, or storm. In a [separate USGS project account](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/great-lakes-restoration-initiative/science/urban-best-management-practices-reporting),
green infrastructure over clay soils in Detroit showed little stormwater-volume
reduction. These findings make soil and drainage conditions part of the local
question. They are external field evidence, not a Turntide field outcome.

# Rainfall evidence still needed

For a United States site, [NOAA's Precipitation Frequency Data Server](https://hdsc.nws.noaa.gov/pfds/)
can provide Atlas 14 precipitation frequency estimates and uncertainty for a
selected location. An estimate can help frame a design question; it is not a
record of a storm at the site or proof that a proposed garden can safely receive
runoff. A local answer still needs an observed drainage path, site conditions,
an appropriate local design guide, and a human decision about any trial. No
location has been selected and no rainfall estimate has been calculated for
this Turntide question. Share only a broad locality in public Discord; discuss
exact site details privately with a consenting organizer if a trial is proposed.
